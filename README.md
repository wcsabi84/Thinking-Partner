What is a genuinely useful way to think about it?
A genuinely useful way to think about it is: don’t ask “Is Bhutan better than Ireland?” Ask “Which set of problems would I rather have?”
Every place gives you something and charges you something. Ireland may charge you with weather, cost, pace, housing, or a feeling of sameness. Bhutan may give you novelty, mountains, quiet and cultural depth—but charge you with fewer career options, distance from family, bureaucracy, weaker healthcare access and being an outsider in a small society.
The most important distinction is moving toward something versus escaping something. If your main thought is “I need to get out of Ireland,” Bhutan can easily become a projection screen. If instead you can say, “I want a smaller society, proximity to mountains, a slower rhythm, less consumption, and I’m willing to trade career optionality and convenience for that,” then you have an actual thesis to test.
I’d use four questions:
1.	What do I want my ordinary Tuesday to look like? Ignore monasteries, trekking and spectacular scenery. Picture grocery shopping, work, friends, healthcare, dating or family life, rainy days, bureaucracy and boredom. Which country gives you the Tuesday you want?
2.	What am I unwilling to sacrifice? Pick perhaps three things: income, proximity to family, career progression, healthcare, nature, community, privacy, freedom to travel, etc. A place that violates one of your true non-negotiables is probably wrong regardless of how attractive it feels.
3.	Is the decision reversible? You don't need to decide whether to “move to Bhutan forever.” A much better decision is: “Is Bhutan interesting enough to justify a serious experiment?” Keep your Irish options intact, go there, and collect evidence. Reversible decisions should be made relatively easily; irreversible ones deserve much more evidence.
4.	What would make the experiment a success or failure? Decide beforehand. For example: “After two months, do I have meaningful relationships? Can I realistically work here? Do I feel more alive in normal life, not just on weekends? Do I still like it after the novelty fades?”
There is also a useful mental test: imagine that nobody would ever know you moved to Bhutan. No interesting story, no Instagram photos, nobody impressed by it. Would you still want the life itself? If yes, that's meaningful.
So I wouldn't frame your current decision as Ireland vs Bhutan. I'd frame it as:
“Do I have enough evidence that the kind of life available in Bhutan fits me better than my current life in Ireland to justify the next reversible step?”
At this stage, the answer may well be “enough evidence to investigate, nowhere near enough evidence to uproot my life.” That is a perfectly substantive conclusion.
What does the latest research actually say about this?
Yes. Having looked at the newer evidence, I’d sharpen what I said earlier. The research does not support the idea that moving to a “happier”, more natural or slower country will reliably make you happier. It supports something more interesting: a move tends to work when it changes the parts of your life that are actually causing the mismatch, while allowing you to build belonging and maintain agency.
The most relevant findings are:
1.	Belonging matters more than cultural similarity. A major 2025 Nature Communications meta-analysis combined 1,114 studies, 5,066 effects and 571,260 first-generation migrants. The strongest correlates of successful adaptation weren't how culturally similar the destination was. They were low acculturative stress, low perceived discrimination, not being lonely, feeling connected, and having social support. The researchers found cultural distance mattered, but considerably less. A separate systematic review of 43 migration studies similarly found social networks, support and community belonging consistently associated with better wellbeing after resettlement, although much of that evidence remains observational. 
2.	Wanting the move yourself appears important. A 2026 longitudinal study followed people before and after an actual residential move. Those who experienced their move as voluntary subsequently showed improvements in life satisfaction, mental health, residential satisfaction and attachment to their new place. That fits a broader psychological literature on person–environment fit: environments matter partly because some environments make it easier for particular people to live in accordance with their needs and characteristics. So “Bhutan fits me” is potentially meaningful; “Bhutan is objectively a better way of life” isn't strongly supported.
3.	Nature really does help—but probably not enough to carry a relocation decision. Recent systematic reviews find reasonably consistent improvements in mood from exposure to natural environments. Results for cognition and physiological stress are less consistent, and studies vary substantially. In other words, living among Himalayan mountains could genuinely improve an aspect of your daily experience if nature is important to you. But research doesn't suggest that scenery compensates for loneliness, occupational frustration or financial insecurity.
4.	Bhutan's “Gross National Happiness” reputation is easy to misunderstand. Bhutan's latest full GNH survey, from 2022, scored the country at 0.781 on its multidimensional index; 48.1% of respondents met its thresholds for “extensively” or “deeply” happy. But GNH includes 33 indicators spanning health, living standards, education, time use, culture, governance, community and ecology—it isn't simply people answering “Are you happy?” Interestingly, Bhutan's own data found urban residents had higher overall GNH than rural residents, including somewhat higher sufficiency in life satisfaction. So the empirical Bhutan is more complicated than the Western image of simple rural Himalayan life producing happiness.
5.	Bhutan itself is experiencing the tension between meaning and opportunity. A September 2026 IMF analysis finds that Bhutan's substantial income growth has translated into only modest increases in measured GNH. It also identifies youth unemployment and substantial outward migration of educated Bhutanese as current structural problems. The 2022 GNH data showed gains in living standards and community vitality but deterioration in culture and time use. That's important evidence against romanticising the country: many Bhutanese value their society enormously while simultaneously leaving it in search of economic and professional opportunities.
And here's a useful comparison. Ireland ranks 13th in the 2026 World Happiness Report, based on average self-reported life evaluations during 2023–2025. That does not mean you personally ought to prefer Ireland. It illustrates why country rankings are a poor decision rule for an individual: national averages can't tell us whether your particular needs are being met.

What does this look like over the next five to ten years, not just today?
Over a five-to-ten-year horizon, I think Bhutan becomes more interesting as a relocation possibility—but also less like the timeless, unchanged Bhutan people often imagine.
The key fact is that Bhutan is entering a deliberate economic transformation. Its current five-year plan aims to roughly expand GDP to US$5 billion by 2029 and ultimately become a high-income “GNH economy” by 2034. The IMF expects medium-term growth around 6.5%, driven initially by hydropower, investment and services. 
That doesn't mean your standard of living would automatically improve. It means that the Bhutan of 2035 could be materially different from the Bhutan you'd move to in 2026.
The biggest wildcard is Gelephu Mindfulness City. This isn't merely a property development: Bhutan has made it a special administrative region with its own emerging commercial, employment, financial and tax legislation. Government planning documents describe 2025–2035 as its initial “foundation” phase, including the city and airport infrastructure. The IMF thinks it has genuine potential to attract foreign investment and diversify Bhutan's economy, while explicitly warning that it also carries substantial execution and financial risks. 
So I'd envisage three possible Bhutans around 2031–36.
The successful-transition Bhutan is considerably easier for an Irish professional to live in than today: better international connections, more high-skilled jobs, more foreign residents, improved infrastructure and services, while still retaining much of Bhutan's distinctive culture and environment. Hydropower provides income while GMC and other industries start producing jobs. Bhutan becomes a small but unusually interesting Asian economy rather than primarily a tourism/hydropower economy.
The muddling-through Bhutan, which I think is at least as plausible, grows strongly on paper but struggles to translate national growth into attractive everyday careers. That problem is already visible: youth unemployment was 20.6% in late 2025, while the World Bank estimates about 9% of Bhutan's population had emigrated by 2025. Hydropower can make GDP look excellent without generating huge numbers of professional jobs. GMC develops, but more slowly than its vision suggests.
And there is a disrupted-transition Bhutan in which hydropower delays, capital constraints, climate events or weaker-than-expected investment derail part of the transformation. Bhutan is particularly exposed to floods, landslides, changing rainfall and glacial hazards; the World Bank estimates roughly 65% of the population is exposed to at least one natural hazard and expects urban areas to face increasing flood, heat and landslide risk. 
Meanwhile, Ireland is unlikely to stand still either. The most useful current long-range ESRI scenario has underlying Irish economic activity—GNI* rather than distorted headline GDP—growing about 2.3% annually through 2030 and 2.1% during 2031–35. That's much slower than Bhutan's projected growth, but from a vastly higher starting point and with substantially deeper institutions and labour markets. 
Ireland's big problem over that horizon remains capacity. Its population is expected to continue growing under all current CSO scenarios, with the population aged 65+ passing one million by 2030. The government now aims to build more than 300,000 homes through 2030; if anything close to that is achieved, today's housing constraints could ease materially, although a target obviously isn't the same thing as delivered housing. Healthcare will simultaneously be under greater demographic pressure—the government reported about 754,000 people on active hospital waiting lists at the end of 2025. 
Climate change isn't a simple Bhutan-bad/Ireland-good comparison either. Ireland is expected to become warmer, with more variable precipitation, wetter winters, drier summers and more heavy-rainfall events, but the physical risks are generally much less acute than Himalayan flooding and landslide risks.

The reason isn't that Bhutan looks worse. The problem is that the current route from “I like Bhutan” to “I have a sustainable life in Bhutan” is much narrower than the route from “I like Bhutan” to “I enjoy visiting Bhutan.” Immigration/employment, healthcare, social integration and career opportunity constitute four separate gates. The things I genuinely didn't expect
The 9% emigration estimate is the biggest one. A country can simultaneously be culturally attractive, rapidly growing and beautiful—and have a large share of its own population deciding that economic opportunity lies elsewhere. That doesn't tell you not to go, but it's powerful counterevidence against a simplistic “Bhutan has discovered a better way of living” narrative.
Second was urban Bhutan outperforming rural Bhutan on Bhutan's own GNH measure. That makes me considerably more interested in Thimphu or future Gelephu as actual life environments than in an ideal of disappearing into rural Bhutan.
Third was how quickly Gelephu has moved from vision to institutional machinery. It is already a 4,046 km² special administrative region with separate executive/legislative powers, its own emerging regulatory framework and active financial-sector licensees. I wouldn't have assigned as much probability to a genuinely novel Bhutanese economic ecosystem emerging over your 5–10-year window before doing this cross-check.
And fourth: the empirical case for belonging is stronger than the empirical case for “Bhutanese lifestyle.” That's perhaps the deepest result. The available migration literature gives us quite a lot of evidence that loneliness, connectedness, discrimination and acculturative stress matter. It gives us much less reason to believe that mountains, slower pace or national philosophies of happiness automatically produce a better life for an immigrant. 
So my present working hypothesis would be:
Bhutan is worth investigating seriously if you are attracted to Bhutanese society itself. It is much less compelling if you're principally attracted to what Bhutan represents.
The next highest-value piece of evidence isn't another macroeconomic report. It's finding out why Bhutan has such a pull on you and whether that survives contact with ordinary Bhutanese life.
What prompt should I have used from the very start, and what would have been the most useful questions to ask?

I’d use this:

1.	What problem are you hoping moving to Bhutan will solve?
2.	Why Bhutan specifically—not just “somewhere different”?
3.	What does your current life in Ireland look like on an ordinary Tuesday, and what do you wish were different about it?
4.	What would an excellent ordinary Tuesday in Bhutan look like?
5.	Which parts of that imagined life actually require Bhutan?
6.	If you could get 80% of that life without leaving Europe, would you still choose Bhutan?
7.	What are you unwilling to sacrifice: relationships, career, income, healthcare, convenience, family proximity, freedom of movement, something else?
8.	What are you unusually willing to sacrifice that most people would not be?
9.	How important is belonging to you, and how good are you at building close relationships from scratch?
10.	Do you want Bhutanese society, or do you want mountains, Buddhism, quiet and escape from Western life?
11.	How ambitious are you professionally, really?
12.	Would Bhutan still appeal if it became richer, busier, more international and less like the image you currently have of it?
13.	What evidence from your past suggests you thrive in unfamiliar places rather than merely enjoy visiting them?
14.	What would make moving there a failure after three years?
15.	If the move failed, how costly would returning to Ireland be?
16.	At age 70, which mistake seems more painful: having tried and come back, or never having seriously tested it?
17.	What answer are you secretly hoping this analysis gives you?
18.	What fact, if true, would make you abandon the Bhutan idea immediately?
19.	What fact, if true, would make you substantially more confident about going?
20.	Are you choosing between Ireland and Bhutan—or between your current self and a different version of yourself?
If I had to reduce even that further, the five questions with the highest information value would be:
What are you actually trying to change?
Why does that require Bhutan?
What would you lose that you currently underestimate?
Can you realistically belong there?
What is the cheapest experiment that could prove you wrong?
Those five would probably have gotten us to the heart of the issue much faster than beginning with visas, GDP, happiness rankings or country comparisons.
